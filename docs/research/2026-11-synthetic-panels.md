# Synthetic consumer panels: what the evidence supports

Researched 2026-11-15 from papers, preprints and method documentation. Basis for a skill that invents N buyers, pitches them an idea, and records BUY or PASS with reasons and a price. Where a claim comes from a secondary summary rather than the primary text, it says so.

## What LLM respondents get right and wrong

The early papers framed LLMs as a cheap pilot tool, not a replacement. [Argyle et al. 2023, "Out of One, Many"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.06899) (Political Analysis, doi 10.1017/pan.2023.2) conditioned GPT-3 on real survey backstories to build "silicon samples" and proposed "algorithmic fidelity", judged on four criteria: a social science Turing test, backward continuity, forward continuity and pattern correspondence (as listed in footnote 7 of Bisbee et al., below). [Aher, Arriaga and Kalai](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.10264) (ICML 2023) replicated the ultimatum game, garden path sentences and Milgram, but found a "hyper-accuracy distortion": in a wisdom-of-crowds task most simulated participants in ChatGPT and GPT-4 gave the exact correct answer to obscure questions, and the authors suspect alignment training. [Horton's "Homo silicus"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.07543) replicated status quo bias and fairness results with GPT-3 and says the most obvious use is "to pilot experiments in silico first", while results "will still require empirical confirmation".

The marketing evidence is mixed, and it got more cautious over time.

- [Brand, Israeli and Ngwe, "Using LLMs for Market Research"](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/23-062_1f58623a-ee21-44b9-a262-276047bc5543.pdf) (HBS working paper 23-062, version of 30 April 2026) found GPT demand slopes downward and responds to income, but baseline willingness to pay (WTP) is often wrong: about 3 times too high for fluoride, almost 3 times too high for a laptop projector, wrong-signed for new toothpaste flavours and for screen size, and it varies across model versions. Fine-tuning on human survey data from the same category fixed several of these, but it made cross-category estimates worse and never recovered differences between income, gender or political groups. Their conclusion: a supplement to human studies, not a substitute. Their [2024 slides](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/AIsraeli_GPT_May2024_AICE_final.pdf) also note GPT "gravitates toward the first option presented".
- [Maier et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.08338v2) (PyMC Labs with Colgate-Palmolive, preprint) used 57 personal care concept surveys with 9,300 US respondents. Asked directly for a 1 to 5 purchase intent rating, GPT-4o and Gemini 2.0 Flash answered mostly 3 and almost never 1 or 5, while humans peaked at 4 and 5 (mean about 4.0). Distributional similarity (1 minus the Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance) was only 0.26 and 0.39. Their fix, semantic similarity rating (SSR), asks for a short free-text reaction, embeds it, and compares it with five anchor statements per scale point (six anchor sets averaged). SSR reached 90% of human test-retest correlation with similarity 0.88 for GPT-4o. Without demographic personas, the distributions still matched but the correlation with real product rankings fell from 92% to 50% and ratings drifted more positive. Gender, region and ethnicity effects were not reproduced, and the anchors were tuned on these same surveys.
- [Arora, Chakraborty and Nishimura](https://www.ebsco.com/articles/marketing/5fca75ba-75b4-5c92-aa68-6abdea7d29f6/ai-human-hybrids-for-marketing-research-leveraging-large-language-models-llms-as-collaborators) (Journal of Marketing 2025) report, through summaries I could not check against the full text, that base GPT-4 synthetic respondents got the direction of means right but had smaller variance and did not recover correlations between variables.

Divergences measured in 2024 to 2026 work:

- Variance collapse. [Bisbee et al.](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/B92267DC26195C7F36E63EA04A47D2FE) (Political Analysis 2024) found ChatGPT means close to the ANES, but a standard deviation of 16.1 against 31.4 for humans, which would shrink a power calculation from 129 to 16 respondents. 48% of regression coefficients differed significantly from the human ones and 32% of those flipped sign. Small wording changes moved the distribution, and the same prompt gave different results between April and July 2023.
- Flattening of groups. [Wang, Morgenstern and Dickerson](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01908) (accepted at Nature Machine Intelligence; 3,200 participants, 16 identities, 4 LLMs) show models both misportray and flatten demographic groups. Raising GPT-4's temperature reached human-level n-gram diversity only at 1.4, where the text became incoherent.
- Order and label artefacts. [Dominguez-Olmedo et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.07951) (NeurIPS 2024) found survey answers driven by option position and labels such as "A"; once order is randomised, answers look close to uniform.
- WEIRD skew. [Atari et al., "Which Humans?"](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/centers/cid/publications/which-humans) report that LLM answers most resemble WEIRD populations and fall away with distance from them (r = -0.70, from the abstract). [Santurkar et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17548) found opinion misalignment with US groups that persists after steering.
- Price confounding. [Gui and Toubia](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.15524v3) show that changing only the price in a prompt also shifts unstated variables (competitor prices, demographics), and GPT-4o-mini produced an inverted-U demand curve. Telling the model the design ("price was drawn at random from a range") cut mean absolute error from 0.532 to 0.397.
- Clustered prices. [Choi et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.03262) compared LLM personas with 522 Korean adults on pay-what-you-want: humans were diverse, LLM prices clustered at a few points, exact individual accuracy stayed below 5%, and chain-of-thought, RAG and few-shot gave no consistent gain.
- Customers who never leave. [Chen 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.20708) (single-author preprint, matchmaking sales chats, 2,790 real conversations) found LLM customer simulators track real buyers but push real non-buyers toward purchase; after a pitch they deliberate 75% of the time against 45% for humans. An explicit instruction that the customer may disengage cut the bias from +0.24 to -0.04.
- Richer personas help, within limits. [Park et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.10109) built agents of 1,052 Americans from two-hour interviews: they reproduced held-out General Social Survey answers at 83% to 86% of the people's own two-week consistency, against 74% for demographics-only agents.

One counterpoint matters for the skill's premise. In Maier et al., with personas, the synthetic panel was not more positive than the humans; the humans themselves stated high intent. Over-stated intent is a human survey problem too, which is why the discounting in the section on stated intent applies to both.

### Persona copying

A simulated respondent written from a persona tends to restate the persona instead of answering the question: Cheng, Piccardi and Yang (CoMPosT, EMNLP 2023) measure this as caricature, an output that exaggerates the persona's defining traits relative to a default response. In a buyer panel the visible form is a reaction that pastes the buyer's written objection, so the pass reason is fixed before the card is read (issue #13: a median 44% of each objection's words reappeared in the reaction). A real respondent never read the persona file, so for people the rate of verbatim runs copied from it is zero by construction; between simulated buyers, stock phrases ("I don't need it") can coincide, so the comparison is against the same reactions with the objections shuffled across buyers. A one-sided permutation p-value counts the shuffles at least as extreme as the observed rate and adds one to both counts, so it is never zero (Phipson and Smyth, "Permutation p-values should never be zero", 2010). No study gives a cut-off for the copy rate itself, so the check tests copying beyond chance and stays advisory until a real panel shows it separates rounds.

## Sycophancy and what reduces it

[Perez et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09251) found larger models more likely to repeat back a user's preferred answer. [Sharma et al., "Towards Understanding Sycophancy"](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.13548v4) showed it across five assistants: feedback on a text gets more positive when the user says they wrote or like it; when challenged with "are you sure?", models changed a correct answer in 32% (GPT-4) to 86% (Claude 1.3) of questions; and a Claude 2 preference model preferred sycophantic over truthful responses 95% of the time on their baseline set. Human preference data rewards agreement, so the bias is trained in. [Wei et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.03958) found instruction tuning and scale increase it and that light fine-tuning on synthetic data reduces it, which a skill cannot use.

Prompt-level techniques, by strength of evidence:

- Hiding who owns the idea has direct support: the Sharma feedback results show stated authorship or liking moves the evaluation. Present the idea as a third party's product, never as the user's.
- Third-person framing has one benchmark. [Hong et al., SYCON Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23840) (Findings of EMNLP 2025) found that asking the model to answer as a named third person raised resistance to stance flips by up to 63.8% in debate, against up to 28% for an explicit "do not be sycophantic" instruction. That is a different task from buying, so treat it as suggestive.
- Independent contexts are supported indirectly. [Weng et al., BenchForm](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.13381v2) (ICLR 2025) found every tested model conformed to a majority in multi-agent settings. Respondents who see each other's answers will converge, so each buyer should decide in isolation.
- Explicit permission to say no has the Chen 2026 result above, and Brand et al. always offer a no-purchase option in their choice prompts.
- Asking for reasons before the decision is mixed. Free-text reactions before a rating help (Maier et al.), but [Turpin et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04388) (NeurIPS 2023) showed chain-of-thought often rationalises an answer pushed by a biasing feature without mentioning it, and Choi et al. found CoT sometimes lowered accuracy. Reasons are useful as data to map, not as proof the decision is sound.
- Forced trade-offs have support from pricing practice rather than from sycophancy studies: choice tasks with an opt-out gave Brand et al. downward-sloping demand, and Gui and Toubia show the competitive context must be stated or the model invents it.
- Higher temperature raises variance (Bisbee et al. document the link) but does not cure flattening (Wang et al.).
- Devil's advocate prompting of the respondents themselves: I found no measured effect for simulated respondents. Treat it as folklore for this use; it fits better as a separate critique step after the panel.

## Stated intent against actual purchase

Stated intent over-predicts. In [Sun and Morwitz 2010](https://english.ckgsb.edu.cn/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/intention.pdf) (International Journal of Research in Marketing), 15.3% of households said they would buy a computer within a year and 5.7% did; for cars, 9.9% stated and 5.0% bought. [Jamieson and Bass 1989](https://scilit.com/publications/74b0748eb375862960b62170438874a1) (Journal of Marketing Research) compared six conversion schemes for the five-point scale; one weights the boxes 75%, 25%, 10%, 5% and 2% ("definitely" down to "definitely not"), as described by [Chandon, Morwitz and Reinartz](https://flora.insead.edu/fichiersti_wp/inseadwp2004/2004-26.pdf). The [Q research guide](https://www.qresearchsoftware.com/market-research-guide-advanced-questions-new-product-development) lists six firms' rules, with the top box counted anywhere from 28% to 100% and "probably" from 0% to 54%, and notes that every rule down-weights. [Juster's 1966](https://www.nber.org/chapters/c1535.pdf) 11-point purchase probability scale (0 to 10, with labelled probabilities) is the usual alternative; secondary sources say it explained about twice the variance of intentions questions, which I could not check in the scanned original. [Wright and MacRae 2007](https://www.econbiz.de/Record/bias-and-variability-in-purchase-intention-scales-wright-malcolm/10007877457) report from two meta-analyses that probability scales did better than intention scales. Morwitz, Steckel and Gupta 2007 (International Journal of Forecasting 23(3), [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/intfor/v23y2007i3p347-364.html)) is the reference on when intentions predict sales; summaries say existing, durable products and short horizons predict better than new ones, but I could not read the paper itself.

For the skill: report raw BUY share, then a discounted estimate with the scheme named, and say that a new product sits at the weak end of every finding above.

## Pricing questions that work in a survey

The Van Westendorp price sensitivity meter ([van Westendorp 1976, ESOMAR Congress](https://www.rdocumentation.org/packages/pricesensitivitymeter/versions/1.3.0/topics/psm_analysis)) asks four open prices, worded by [Conjointly](https://conjointly.com/products/van-westendorp/) as: so cheap you would doubt the quality (too cheap); a bargain (cheap); getting expensive but still worth thinking about (expensive); so expensive you would not consider it (too expensive). From per-respondent answers:

1. Drop respondents whose answers are not ordered too cheap < cheap < expensive < too expensive.
2. For each candidate price p, compute the share whose too-cheap answer is at or above p, and the same for cheap; compute the share whose expensive answer is at or below p, and the same for too expensive. "Not cheap" is 1 minus cheap and "not expensive" is 1 minus expensive.
3. Point of marginal cheapness: too cheap crosses not cheap. Point of marginal expensiveness: too expensive crosses not expensive. The acceptable range runs between them. Optimal price point: too cheap crosses too expensive. Indifference point: cheap crosses expensive. Some firms use a narrower range (too cheap against expensive, too expensive against cheap), per the R package [pricesensitivitymeter](https://www.rdocumentation.org/packages/pricesensitivitymeter/versions/1.3.0/topics/psm_analysis).
4. The Newton, Miller and Smith extension adds a five-point purchase question at each person's cheap and expensive price, maps it to probabilities (Sawtooth template defaults 0.7, 0.5, 0.3, 0.1, 0), interpolates, and gives a reach-maximising and a revenue-maximising price.

Conjointly warns the curves are cumulative frequencies, not demand, and that the method is weak for entirely new products; [Sawtooth](https://sawtoothsoftware.com/resources/blog/posts/van-westendorp-pricing-sensitivity-meter) calls reading prices off the intersections somewhat like reading tea leaves.

[Gabor-Granger](https://conjointly.com/products/gabor-granger/) asks yes or no at a sequence of prices (up after a yes, down after a no) until each respondent's highest accepted price is found. Demand at p is the share whose highest accepted price is at least p; revenue is p times demand, and its peak is the revenue-maximising price. Conjointly notes understated WTP and overstated intent, which roughly offset in humans; for LLMs Brand et al. found WTP overstated two to threefold, so treat synthetic prices as an upper bound.

## Cheap real-world validation to end with

- The Mom Test ([Fitzpatrick](https://www.momtestbook.com/); rules as summarised by [Unusual Ventures](https://www.unusual.vc/rob-fitzpatricks-mom-test/)): talk about their life, not your idea; ask about specifics in the past; talk less and listen more; discount compliments and "would" statements; count only commitments of time, reputation or money.
- Smoke test or video MVP ([Ries on Dropbox, TechCrunch 2011](https://techcrunch.com/2011/10/19/dropbox-minimal-viable-product/)): a demo video took the beta waiting list from 5,000 to 75,000 overnight, by Houston's account.
- Fake door and Mechanical Turk pretotypes (Savoia, as described in [Berkeley SCET's guide](https://scet.berkeley.edu/pretotyping-for-sportstech-innovation-a-practical-guide/feed/)): offer the thing before it exists and measure who acts; tell people afterwards it was a demand test.
- Pre-sales: ask for a deposit or a signed letter of intent; the Mom Test treats money as the strongest commitment.
- Concierge MVP ([Ries, via Shortform](https://www.shortform.com/blog/concierge-mvp/)): Food on the Table's founder delivered the service by hand to early customers before writing the software.

## A known answer to check against

Sarstedt et al. (below) recommend benchmarking a silicon sample against humans. Before any real test, one human fact sits inside every panel: buyers who already pay for a subscription mostly keep it. [Recurly's churn benchmarks](https://recurly.com/research/churn-rate-benchmarks) (seen 2026-11-16, figures updated with July 2026 data) give a median churn of 3.22% for SaaS businesses and 3.44% for business and professional services; the page calls these annual rates and its body text gives slightly different figures (3.04% and 3.21%), while secondary write-ups quote Recurly as 3% to 5% a month. Read either way, more than nine in ten paying subscribers are still paying a month later. A panel whose paying buyers walk away from the tools they already pay for at many times that rate has over-corrected for the push to purchase (Chen 2026), and its BUY share for a new offer is a floor, not a reading. The first real run of buyer-panel showed this: 0 of 46 buyers who paid for a tool chose any paid option (issue #13 in the supah-skills repository).

## How to present a synthetic panel honestly

The sources agree. Horton and [Sarstedt et al. 2024](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/116232/1/Psychology_and_Marketing_-_2024_-_Sarstedt_-_Using_large_language_models_to_generate_silicon_samples_in_consumer_and.pdf) (Psychology and Marketing) place silicon samples in pretesting and pilots, recommend benchmarking against humans, discarding samples that show no variation, and saving prompts, model versions and outputs. Brand et al. call it a supplement to human studies. Bisbee et al. warn about reliability and reproducibility. The [Insights Association](https://www.insightsassociation.org/News-Updates/Articles/ArticleID/2614/Code-in-Action-Synthetic-Data-Revisited) (June 2026) says synthetic participants should be fully disclosed and not presented as equivalent to human responses without validation. The 2025 ICC/ESOMAR Code reportedly covers synthetic data and personas; I did not read the clause text.

For the skill, the output should say in its first line that the panel is simulated, name the model, frame findings as hypotheses to test, and end with the validation step.
